## The layout change (read this first)

The existing bathroom is too narrow for a clean fixture line-up — shower spray hits the toilet area. Fix: extend the bathroom outward into the living room and re-arrange the fixtures along one wall.

### Wall move

- **The new bathroom entrance wall lines up flush with the existing living-room wall** (continuous flat wall plane — no protrusion). Measured distance: **1003 mm** outward.
- **Resulting interior dimensions: 1350 mm wide × 3000 mm long** (wall-to-wall, both measured on site). The 3 m is the long axis along which the fixtures line up.
- Demolish the existing entrance wall (full height, full width); build the new entrance wall on the flush line with the new door framed in.
- The **bedroom-bathroom dividing wall stays untouched** — future home of the wall-hung shelves; see [BEDROOM](BEDROOM.md).

### New door — reuse the salvaged bedroom door, with louvres cut in

- The **new bathroom door is the existing 800 mm bedroom door**, salvaged from the [BEDROOM](BEDROOM.md) Phase 02 demolition (both old bedroom doors come out to form the 2 m opening; one is reused here).
- Re-hang in the new entrance wall, in a **new wooden frame** (matching the existing wooden door frames elsewhere in the house) sized to suit. **Fully painted** on both sides to protect against moisture — accepted tradeoff for using a wooden frame in a bathroom; the paint coverage is what makes it OK.
- **Hinge side: LEFT, when facing the bathroom from the living room.** Door swings **inward** into the bathroom, panel coming to rest against the left wall when fully open.
- The door frame is mounted on the **existing structural column** at the doorway, which **protrudes ~100 mm** into the bathroom. Frame the salvaged door against this column — don't try to remove or re-route it; it's load-bearing.
- **Cut a louvre vent into the salvaged door** (similar to the existing bathroom door's louvre, which is being discarded). The louvre is essential — see "Ventilation strategy" below.
- The existing louvre-vented bathroom door is removed and discarded.
- **Lock: re-use the salvaged door's existing key-based lock as-is.** No new privacy thumbturn needed.
- **Known limitation — accepted:** the salvaged bedroom door is an interior-grade door, not a bathroom-grade one. Over time it will absorb moisture from showers and degrade (warping, paint blistering, eventual softening of the lower edge). This is an accepted trade-off for the cost saving and material reuse. Plan to replace with a proper bathroom door in the future when it deteriorates — **not in current scope**.
- **DIY — Joe + Mon: paint the bathroom-facing side of the door with the same waterproof paint** used on the painted walls. This won't make it bathroom-grade, but it meaningfully slows moisture absorption and extends the door's life. Living-room-facing side stays in normal interior paint.

### Ventilation strategy — passive, driven by AC overpressure

The rest of the house is **sealed** (the 5 main windows are screwed shut per [WINDOWS-01](WINDOWS-01.md)), so the air conditioning gently **over-pressures** the living-room / bedroom interior. That pressure differential is the bathroom's exhaust:

1. AC overpressure pushes air **through the new door's louvres** into the bathroom.
2. The bathroom **window stays operable** and is **kept open** during normal use, so air flows straight out.
3. Continuous low-flow exhaust → the bathroom doesn't steam up after a shower. **No extractor fan needed.**

For this to work:

- **Bathroom window stays operable, with its existing fly screen.** Already covered by [WINDOWS-01](WINDOWS-01.md) (window untouched in Phase 01). Do not seal it during this Phase 02 work either.
- **No ceiling/wall extractor fan** — not needed, not in scope.

### New fixture order, going in from the new door

Fixtures line up along one wall, in this sequence:

1. **Basin** — immediately on entering, by the door.
2. **Toilet** — middle, between basin and shower.
3. **Shower** — at the back, with the new glass partition.

Effect: shower spray points **away from** the toilet (toilet sits *beside* the shower, not in the spray line). The glass partition contains splashes.

## What gets done

### Demolition

- Existing entrance wall (full height) — demolish.
- All existing wall + floor tiles — strip out.
- Existing bathroom door + frame (louvre-vented) — remove and discard.
- Existing toilet pan, basin, basin taps — remove.
- Existing shower head, mixer, water heater, and surface plumbing — disconnect, **keep the units**, re-route the pipework cleanly (see "Plumbing tidy" + "Shower").

### Build the new entrance wall

- Build new wall **1003 mm** further outward into the living room than the existing entrance wall (flush with the existing living-room wall plane).
- Frame the salvaged 800 mm bedroom door into the new wall.
- Tile-ready substrate on the bathroom side; finished + paint-ready on the living-room side. (Joe + Mon paint the living-room side themselves.)

### Suspended ceiling gap — fix at the new wall line

Moving the bathroom entrance wall outward leaves a **gap in the existing suspended ceiling** where the old wall used to meet it, and again where the new wall now meets it. Both transitions need to be sealed.

- **Patch the ceiling so it reads as continuous across the new bathroom area.** Either extend the existing ceiling style (suspended grid + tiles, or whatever finish is in place) the extra 1003 mm so the new bathroom has the same ceiling as before, or cap the bathroom ceiling cleanly at the new wall line — builder's call based on what's economical and what the existing construction allows.
- The fix must leave **no visible gap, no dropped tiles, no exposed grid** at either the old wall line (now demolished) or the new wall line.
- Existing ceiling light fitting position is unchanged — see "Electrical" below — so the patch must work around it.

### No skirting — flush floor-to-wall

- **No skirting boards anywhere** in the bathroom (tiled walls or painted walls).
- Floor tiles meet the wall tile or paint **flush**, with a clean silicone bead at the floor-to-wall junction.
- This is deliberate — keeps the bathroom looking clean, makes the floor easier to clean, avoids the dated-skirting look.

### Floor levels — keep the existing 50 mm step

**Hard constraint: no slab work** — no digging, no building up. Levels stay as today. Both zones graded to drain via thin mortar bed under the new tiles, not by cutting or building the slab.

- **Wet zone** (shower + toilet, back): existing lower slab + new tiles. Falls to the existing drain via the new tile bed.
- **Basin / entry zone** (front, by the new door): existing higher slab + new tiles, **already at living-room floor level** (so no step at the door). Graded falling toward the wet zone so splashes run off, no pooling. Includes the 1003 mm strip absorbed from the living room — same level, uniform finished floor across the whole dry zone.
- **The 50 mm step is INSIDE the bathroom**, between the two zones. Not at the door. We inherit it.
- **Clean step edge** at the transition: single straight line, square edge, visible tile lip. No taper, no curve.
- No door threshold to detail — bathroom and living-room tile meet level at the doorway with a clean grout/silicone line.

### New floor drain — same location, new grate

- The **existing floor-drain location is unchanged.** Reuse the existing drain outlet — no re-plumbing of the soil/waste line for the drain.
- Fit a **new grate** over it: standard square stainless or chrome. **Basic — nothing fancy.** Not a designer linear drain.
- **Floor falls (slope) toward this drain** across the wet zone. Builder grades the wet-zone slab to give a clean run-off to the existing drain location with no pooling.

### Waterproofing — wet-zone floor only

Because the house is masonry (single-brick walls + concrete slab), full sheet membrane is **not** in scope. The decision is:

- **Wet-zone floor:** **liquid-applied waterproof coating** (e.g., SCG Aquapanel paint or any cement-based waterproof slurry). Painted onto the slab in the wet zone before tiling. This is the highest-risk surface — water pools and tracks toward the drain — and the coating is the cheap insurance against water seeping under the tiles, across the slab, and surfacing as damp at the base of the new entrance wall on the living-room side.
- **Tiled walls (shower area + basin/toilet wall):** **no membrane.** Rely on tile + waterproof grout + silicone at all internal corners. Defensible on a masonry wall.
- **Painted walls and the rest of the floor:** no waterproofing — they're not wet zones.
- **No sheet membrane anywhere.**

### Plumbing tidy

The current exposed pipes (water-heater hoses, shower flex across the wall, supply runs) look messy. Fix:

- **Chase all supply / waste runs into the masonry** behind where new tiles will go. **Default = chase**, not surface-mounted runs. Result: when new tiles are laid, there's nothing surface-mounted on the wall.
- **Exception: the basin trap stays exposed** — a proper visible S-bend (or P-bend) below the basin, for cleaning access. Do **not** chase the basin trap into the wall. Use stainless-steel finish on the exposed bottle-trap / S-bend to match the basin taps and the rest of the bathroom hardware.
- Existing fittings stay in their (slightly re-positioned) locations — only the *paths* of the supply lines change.
- The new toilet position needs **basic plumbing re-routing** to reach the new soil-pipe spigot and supply line. Standard work, in scope.

**Considered and rejected: concealed plumbing wall.** A dedicated stud-built "wet wall" running in front of the basin / toilet / shower wall — concealing all supply lines and giving a fully flush tiled surface — was considered and **explicitly rejected**. Reasons: huge cost addition, eats bathroom width (already tight), and harder pipe access for future repairs. **Do not quote this as the default approach**; chase + rigid-runs is the spec.

### Shower — moved ~200 mm left + new glass partition

- The **existing shower unit** is the chosen permanent shower — bought new ~1 month ago and **all stainless steel**. It sets the metal-finish theme for the whole bathroom (basin taps, bidet sprayer, basin trap S-bend, towel rail all match it).
- Move this **existing shower head ~200 mm to the left** of its current position so it sits cleanly in the new wet-zone layout. Re-route supply pipes accordingly.
- Existing water heater + shower mixer **kept** (no new units), but the **water heater shifts a similar ~200 mm** along the wall to follow the moved shower head — so the supply runs to the heater stay short and clean. Shower mixer either follows similarly or stays put; builder calls on site for the cleanest plumbing run.
- **Maintenance note (Joe + Mon, ongoing):** all-stainless hardware shows water marks and needs frequent wiping down to stay looking nice. Accepted trade-off for the consistent stainless look.
- **New shower glass partition: single piece, ~500 mm wide × 2100 mm high.** Frameless toughened glass. Splash guard between the wet zone and the toilet — not a full enclosure.
- **Fixed both at the floor and at the wall** via **aluminium U-profiles chased into the wall and floor (flush, recessed)** — not surface-mounted brackets. The glass slides into the chased profiles. Result: the glass reads as if it grows out of the wall and floor, no visible bracketry — clean modern look.
- **Walk-in: no shower curb.** Toilet and shower share the same wet-zone floor level. Water is contained by the shower glass + the floor slope to the existing drain. Do **not** build a raised curb/threshold under the shower glass — flat floor straight through.

### Toilet — moved between new basin and shower

- New toilet pan in the new position (middle of the line, between basin and shower).
- Re-route soil pipe and supply line to reach the new position.
- **Pan + cistern + seat as a set — cheap and standard, COMPACT projection.** Low-cost spec.
  - **Pick a compact close-coupled model: ≤ 600 mm projection from wall.** Standard pans run 650–700 mm; in a 1350 mm wide bathroom that leaves only 650 mm clear in front (borderline). A **compact 600 mm pan** gives a comfortable 750 mm clear. Common at Cotto, American Standard, HomePro under "compact" or "small bathroom" lines.
  - **Style: modern, not traditional.** Clean geometric / squared-off ceramic. **No floral patterns, no period mouldings, no old-style scrollwork.** Plain white ceramic.
  - Close-coupled cistern (cistern bolts onto the back of the pan). **Single-flush is fine** — water is cheap in Thailand, no need to pay extra for dual-flush.
  - **Standard plastic seat — no soft-close, no bidet seat, no fancy hinges.** Cheap.
  - Pick a model where the soil-pipe spigot spacing matches what the new layout sets out, so no extra plumbing work.
- **New stainless-steel bidet sprayer** ("bum gun") mounted next to the toilet, fed off the cold supply. Stainless to match the shower hardware and the new basin taps — consistent metal finish across the bathroom.

### Basin + taps — moved to the new position by the door

- New basin in the new position (immediately on entering, by the door).
- Wall-hung white ceramic basin.
- **Width: up to 600 mm.** Pick any model in the 450–600 mm range; the layout (see "Fixture spacing" above) accommodates the full 600 mm without forcing a specific brand. Projection from wall: ~350–400 mm typical for wall-hung; leaves ~950 mm clear in front (1350 mm bathroom width minus basin projection).
- **Style: modern, not traditional.** Clean geometric form (rectangular or simple rounded — not floral, not pedestal-style with old-school detailing). Plain white. **Cheap and standard, but the look must read as modern.** Match the toilet's visual style.
- **Taps: stainless steel** to match the existing stainless steel shower hardware.
- Re-route supply + waste to reach the new position.

### Big fixed wall mirror (above basin + toilet)

A single large fixed mirror across the basin/toilet wall, **butting hard against the shower glass partition on one end and the new entrance wall on the other**. Looks generous and "big bathroom" with no visible mirror edges or gaps.

- **Both vertical ends butt cleanly:** one end butts against the **shower glass partition**, the other end butts against the **new entrance wall**. No exposed mirror edges, no edge profiles, no decorative trim — just a clean butt joint with a silicone bead.
- **Width:** the full distance between those two vertical surfaces — i.e., the full length of the basin + toilet wall.
- **Top edge:** 2100 mm above floor — same height as the top of the shower glass partition, so the two visually align across the wall.
- **Bottom edge: 1000 mm above the finished floor.** The basin top sits **just above** this line, so the mirror-to-tile junction at the bottom is **hidden behind the basin** — clean look, no visible silicone bead at the mirror's lower edge. Pick the basin model to suit (basin rim a touch above 1000 mm).
- Accepted trade-off: the mirror will catch some splash from basin use and will need wiping down. That's fine — the look is worth it.
- Frameless, toughened/safety glass, plain (no LED, no cabinet, not heated).
- Fixed flat against the new wall tiles behind it.

### New tiles — two walls + the whole floor

**Tiled walls (full height to 2100 mm AFL):**

1. **Fixture wall** — the long wall the basin / toilet / shower sit against (3.0 m × 2.1 m). Tiled across its full length so the mirror behind the basin/toilet meets continuous tile, and the shower glass meets continuous tile.
2. **Back wall of the wet zone** — the 1.35 m end wall opposite the door, behind the shower.

**Not tiled** — cabinet wall (the long wall opposite the fixture wall), entry/door wall, anything above 2100 mm AFL. See "Painted walls" below.

**Floor:** new tiles across **the whole bathroom** — both the wet zone (lower) and the basin/entry zone (50 mm higher).

**One tile range** across the tiled walls + the floor. Pemo Beige (selected here) is R11 anti-slip everywhere, so no separate wet-zone variant is needed.

**Tile look — modern, low-cost:**

- **Colour: beige** (Pemo Beige, glazed porcelain). Plain — not patterned, not floral.
- **Edge: rectified** — sharp machined edges, enables tight 2 mm grout lines.
- **Grout colour:** match the tile (beige), not contrasting dark grout.

**Tile-to-paint junction: clean seal, no trim.** Clean grout edge + silicone bead — no Schluter / steel / aluminium edge profile.

## Steps (order of work)

The order matters — out-of-sequence work creates rework. Builder follows roughly:

1. **Strip out.** Remove existing wall + floor tiles, the old louvre door, the old toilet pan, the old basin + taps, the existing mirror cabinet. **Disconnect — do not dispose of** — the existing shower head, mixer, and water heater.
2. **Demolish the existing entrance wall** down to slab.
3. **Build the new entrance wall** at the flush-with-living-room-wall line, framed for the salvaged 800 mm bedroom door (door not yet hung).
4. **Suspended ceiling gap fix** at both the old wall line (now demolished) and the new wall line, so the ceiling reads continuous across the new bathroom area.
5. **Plumbing rough-in.** Chase pipes into masonry; re-route the shower head ~200 mm left and the water heater to follow; re-route the toilet's soil + supply lines to the new toilet position; re-route basin supply + waste to the new basin position; tap off cold supply for the new bidet sprayer at the new toilet position.
6. **Electrical first-fix.** Run cable for the new inside light switch and the new IP-rated outlet beside the basin. Fit back-boxes. Don't fit faceplates yet.
7. **Floor work — no slab changes.** Grade the wet-zone tile bed toward the existing drain. Grade the basin/entry-zone tile bed falling toward the wet zone. Both levels stay as they currently are — no digging, no building up, just thin-mortar/thinset under the new tiles to set the falls.
8. **Liquid-applied waterproof coating** onto the wet-zone floor only. Cure per product spec.
9. **Wall tiles** on the two tiled walls (shower-area walls + basin/toilet wall), full height.
10. **Floor tiles** across both zones; clean square step edge at the 50 mm drop; anti-slip variant in the wet zone; same range as the wall tiles where finishes match.
11. **Grout + silicone.** Wet edges, internal corners, tile-to-paint junctions (clean grout + silicone bead, no edge profiles).
